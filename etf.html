How the numbers are calculated

Returns, risk-free rate and beta. The ETF is shorted at the first close of the window (since inception, 3 years, 1 year, or from the date you pick, moved to the first trading day on or after it) and held to the latest close, using Yahoo's dividend-adjusted (total return) prices. R_X and R_SPY are the ETF's and SPY's annualized returns over those dates. RFR is the 13-week Treasury bill yield (Yahoo ^IRX, or FRED's 3-month bill rate if Yahoo has none), accrued every day on actual/360 over the same dates and annualized the same way. Beta is the ETF's beta to SPY from the last year of daily returns (covariance ÷ variance, on days both traded), whatever window is selected; with 60–119 days of history it's marked with *, and with fewer there's no beta.

Break-even fees. A short earns the risk-free rate on its proceeds, so shorting SPY gives −1.00 beta at a hedge cost of RFR − R_SPY a year. Shorting the ETF at fee f gives −β at RFR − R_X − f; levered up to −1.00 beta (× 1/β), that's (RFR − R_X − f) ÷ β.

  • Short profits, after RFR: f = RFR − R_X. Levering up doesn't move where profit crosses zero, so beta doesn't enter.
  • Beta-adjusted vs shorting SPY: f = β × (R_SPY − RFR) − (R_X − RFR), the fee at which the levered short costs the same per unit of beta as shorting SPY.

Example: SPY +10% a year and RFR 4%, so shorting SPY costs −6% a year per −1.00 beta. An ETF with beta 0.2 returning +5% costs −1% a year at no fee, or −5% levered ×5, 1 point cheaper than SPY. Its break-even fee is 0.2 × 6% − 1% = 0.2% a year.

Verdict. Worthwhile: the quoted fee is at or below the beta-adjusted break-even. Profits, costlier hedge than SPY: below the short-profits break-even only. Not worthwhile: above both. Betas under 0.1 (or no beta) aren't treated as a SPY hedge. Each ETF's details also show the previous, unadjusted break-evens, which charge borrow daily on the short's current value (360-day year): short profits −R ÷ A, short beats owning SPY (−R − R_SPY) ÷ A and short ETF / long SPY (R_SPY − R) ÷ A, where A is the sum of the short's value relative to its start × days ÷ 360. Margin costs, IBKR's spread on interest, recalls and fee changes are left out.

Theoretical fee. Every position in the ETF's latest holdings report, weighted by its exposure as a share of net assets (not re-scaled to 100%), times its borrow fee, summed:

  • Stocks and ETFs: the IBKR fee, matched by ISIN, CUSIP, ticker, IBKR's non-US country files, or as a last resort a unique exact company-name match.
  • Swaps and futures: exposure is the notional amount ÷ net assets, at the fee of the stock they track. Index swaps use the components listed in the filing, else the fund's own stock holdings as a stand-in for the index, else an ETF that holds the index (for example SOXX for semiconductors). Options use delta-adjusted exposure. So a 2x single-stock ETF comes out at about 2 × that stock's fee.
  • Short exposure (inverse funds, written calls) needs no borrow: 0%.
  • Cash, T-bills, Treasuries, money market funds and repo aren't borrowed to replicate the fund: they're charged the "fee for cash & T-bills" above (0% by default).
  • Bonds and anything unmatched are charged the "fee for unmatched holdings" above. Rate, currency, credit and commodity derivatives count as 0%.

Sources. Quoted fees, rebates and availability: Interactive Brokers' public stock loan files, refreshed about every 15 minutes. Holdings: the ETF's latest SEC N-PORT filing (complete, but filed quarterly and published up to 60 days later), or Yahoo's top 10 holdings when no filing is found.
